One hit, four buildings

Everything below follows from a single problem: the same performance is a different record in a different room. Waits solved that problem by moving until it sounded right, and the moving was frequently more decisive than the playing. Strike the source, change the building, listen to what the walls do to it.

Short lead-in

What had already changed

The pivot happened nine years before Bone Machine, and it is worth exactly one paragraph here because the rest of this piece is about rooms and hardware. Waits married Kathleen Brennan in 1980. By Swordfishtrombones in 1983 she had opened up his listening to Captain Beefheart, to Harry Partch, to field recordings and language records and things that did not sound like records at all, and she pushed him to produce himself. The version of Waits who walks into Prairie Sun in 1991 is already the one who believes a song is a location before it is a chord chart.

What arrives with Bone Machine is the harder rule underneath that: get the room right and the performance follows. On Bone Machine he found the room and built the drums out of whatever was leaning against the wall. On Real Gone, twelve years later, he arrived with the rhythm already recorded on a cassette in his own house, and the studio's only job was to not sand it down. Same principle. Opposite direction.

The stories took over, so there is more bone than machine on it. Waits, paraphrasing his own abandoned concept · Bone Machine press kit, Island, 1992 confirmed
Site survey

Two buildings that were not studios

Read this pair across, not down. Almost every difference between the two records is visible in the floor plan.

Prairie Sun Recording, Cotati CA

A working chicken ranch before it was a studio. Waits had booked the real tracking room, Studio B, heard the first playback and did not want it. He rented microphones and preamps and tried recording at his own house instead; that did not work either. Back at Prairie Sun he walked the property, stopped at a junk-filled cement storage building down the drive and asked what it would sound like emptied out.reported

They emptied it. Concrete floor, redwood walls and roof, a small footprint with a surprisingly tall ceiling, a hot water heater in the corner, old-growth timber at least sixty years old. It was not soundproofed. It rained through the sessions and the cable runs went down the hill in the wet. The space did not exist as a recording room before this record; it has been called the Waits Room ever since.

A vacant schoolhouse, Sacramento Delta

Widely misreported at the time as the Mississippi Delta. Waits corrected it: the Sacramento Delta, near his own house in Sonoma County. An old school building, empty, chosen for the same reason as the hatchery: he was looking for somewhere with an unusual sound and no studio politics.confirmed

Mark Howard recorded and mixed it. Howard's practice is to carry his own rig and install it fresh in whatever building the record is happening in, then strip it out afterwards, so the schoolhouse became a studio for the length of the sessions and then stopped being one. The decisive detail is where he sat: in the room, next to Waits, rather than behind control-room glass.

Bone Machine · 1992

Hitting things that had never been hit

The record's pulse is a man alone on a cement floor with a small kit, a pile of scrap metal and a bass player for company. The songs were mostly written that way too: a drum, a voice, and no instrument capable of suggesting a chord.

Waits has been consistent about the writing method. Most of Bone Machine came out of a room with a drum in it and him shouting over the top, which is why so much of the album sits on one or two harmonic centres and moves by rhythm and register instead of changes. He described the songs of that period as three-legged chairs: made fast, structurally wrong, and stable anyway if you sit in them properly. This was the first Waits album where he played a serious amount of the percussion himself, and he has said plainly that he likes to play drums when he is angry, which tells you most of what you need to know about the take selection.

Things that have never been hit before. Waits on his choice of percussion · Bone Machine press kit, Island, 1992 confirmed

The signal path

The physical problem with the hatchery was that it was fifty yards from anything that could record. The solution shaped the sound: get a preamp as close to the microphone as possible, then send an already-amplified signal down a long buried line to the console rather than sending a fragile mic-level signal the whole way.

The instrument

An iron crucifix with a hardware store hanging off it

Waits's neighbour Serge Etienne, a sculptor and welder who built motorcycles down the road, made him a percussion rack and gave it to him. Waits called it the Conundrum. He has described it as looking like a big iron crucifix, or a Chinese torture device, with crowbars and Tijuana sabers and found metal hung off the frame, and said the sound is a jail door closing behind you.

Dispatches from the cement room

Earth Died Screaming

The best single illustration of the whole method. Jim Jarmusch had posted Waits a field recording of pygmy stick percussion and Waits wanted that sound. They tried sticks everywhere inside the room: different positions, different microphones, different sizes and species of stick. Nothing worked. They walked outside, put a microphone on the asphalt of the driveway, and it was immediately there.confirmed

Roughly nine people, including whoever happened to be walking past, hitting 2x4s and firewood on the blacktop. Les Claypool on bass, Chamberlin, and a low unison vocal overdub on top. Waits later said the experience taught him why there are no drums in church.

In the Colosseum

The one released appearance of the Conundrum. Brain on drums. The arrangement is essentially a beating administered in tempo, which is what the rack is for.

Such a Scream

Carries the album's densest layer of found and manipulated noise. Waits wanted even more of it on the track; Brennan wanted less, and the dispute was settled over a hand of cards. He has said the texture sounds like it was made in hell, which he meant as an endorsement.confirmed

Dirt in the Ground

The title is a line the jazz saxophonist Teddy Edwards used to use on women in hotel lobbies. Ralph Carney's bass clarinet and saxes supply the low Ellington-shaped voicings that make the track feel like a funeral band rather than a rock band. An entire verse about Mata Hari was cut for length.

The Ocean Doesn't Want Me

Built from a local newspaper report of a woman found floating. The coined word in it, strange angels compressed into one, came from Waits's daughter Kellesimone. Chamberlin again.

I Don't Wanna Grow Up

Waits wanted to throw it away. Brennan made him finish it. In the room it changed character entirely once they put it through a Marshall and turned it up, which is the whole record's logic applied to a song that could otherwise have been folk.

That Feel

Written with Keith Richards, and by Waits's account genuinely written together rather than assembled from parts: Waits mostly on drums, Richards mostly on guitar. Waits described Richards's working method as circling a song and then taking the eyes out of it. Mixed separately from the rest of the album, in New York.

A Little Rain

Brennan brought the Irish-shaped melody. Waits's phrase for his own contribution was that he saved it from the fire. Dedicated to a dump-truck-driving friend named Clyde.

Whistle Down the Wind

Dedicated to the songwriter Tom Jans, a friend of both Waits and Brennan, dead since 1984. David Hidalgo's violin and accordion are the only conventionally pretty sounds allowed anywhere near the record.

Goin' Out West

Waits's own description amounts to three chords played very loud, which is accurate and is also the point. The guitar tone is doing the work the lyric is too deadpan to do.

Real Gone · 2004

The rhythm section arrived on a cassette

Twelve years later the principle is unchanged and the execution is inverted. Nothing gets struck. Waits made the beats in his own bathroom, late at night, with his mouth, and the band's job was to play over a recording of his own breathing.

He has described the setup with unusual specificity: a four-track cassette machine, a Shure SM58, a very small tiled room with a long slap on it, and the input driven well past where it was supposed to go. He built cycles out of mouth rhythms and then played along with them. Then he overdubbed pots and pans on top of the grunting. Mark Howard, who recorded the album, tried to re-record those parts properly in the schoolhouse and could not beat them. Overloading the machine's preamp was producing a kick-drum thump that a kick drum was not producing, and the degraded quality was not a compromise, it was the sound.confirmed

Waits was careful to say these were not loops in the sequencer sense. The parts were played, repeatedly, by a person, because loops start feeling like wallpaper. And he was blunt about why anyone beatboxes at all: it is cheaper than hiring a drummer, you put a microphone to your mouth and overload something. He rated his own technique as crude and unprofessional.

I'm more like a rhythm guitar. Waits on his own mouth percussion · San Diego Union-Tribune, September 2004 confirmed
The instrument

Build the bathroom take

Start with the mouth kick alone and add the layers in the order he did. Then turn the overload off, which is the one control that matters, and hear the record fall apart.

The signal path

An entire studio, installed and then removed

Howard does not book rooms, he builds them. For Real Gone that meant carrying a hard-disk recorder, a set of preamps and a microphone collection into an empty school and wiring the building up. He is on record as disliking the dominant digital workstation of the period on both sound and reliability grounds, and his phrase for its users is that they listen with their eyes.

The discovery of the sessions was a beaten-up RCA 44 ribbon microphone from the 1940s, borrowed out of Capitol. They had been getting a good vocal sound from a Sony C-37A. Howard put the 44 up and the difference was not incremental.

It just sounded like cream. Mark Howard on the RCA 44 on Waits's voice · Tape Op #134 confirmed
The harmony

The piano that became an end table

For the first time in his career there are no keyboards. He had the piano moved into the schoolhouse and it was never played. People put drinks on it, then a coat, and eventually he could not see it. He offered no theory for why it did not work; it just didn't.

His stated rule is the opposite of minimalism: bring hundreds of instruments, because the one you leave at home is the one you will need. The piano failing the audition is not an aesthetic manifesto, it is an outcome. But the consequence is structural. With no keyboard there is nothing on the record filling in the middle of the chord. Bass states a root, guitars state intervals and noise, and the space between them stays empty, which is why the album sounds like it has holes in it even when six people are playing.

Dispatches from the schoolhouse

Top of the Hill

Opens the record by putting the thesis first: a live band and a turntable playing along to a recording of Waits's voice doing percussion. Casey scratching, Ribot on a single-line figure, nothing underneath but the cassette.

Shake It

The rhythm was an accident with a microphone. A mic left live in the bathroom picked up someone using it and then the door slamming. Waits asked what the sound was, and when Howard explained, told him to get somebody to slam the door and hit the wall with a piece of wood. Brain slammed the door and hit the wall, a kick drum was played against it, and that became the track.confirmed

That's how Tom hears stuff.Mark Howard · SoundStage! Xperience
Hoist That Rag

Ribot plays it in the Cuban register he had already explored on his own records, over Claypool's bass and layered hand percussion. His solo runs something near ninety seconds: states a theme, then works the flat ninth against the harmony until the tension stops being decorative. Horns on top. Waits's summary of Ribot is that you never have to ask him to play like his hair is on fire.

Sins of My Father

Over ten minutes on a rock-steady pulse that Larry Taylor initially refused to play on genre grounds. Brain covers multiple hand drums. A sizzle cymbal is struck once, around four minutes in, and never again. Waits's stated position on this kind of arrangement is that silence is the essential part and he is always more interested in what is not there.

Clang Boom Steam

Effectively the raw bathroom recording with a perfunctory blues lyric laid over it. It exists so the method is audible undisguised at least once on the record.

Metropolitan Glide

Turntables and mouth percussion as the primary rhythm architecture. The dance-instruction lyric is a nineteenth-century form running on a twenty-first century rhythm bed.

Day After Tomorrow

The closer, and the album's one unarmoured moment: a soldier's letter home, written as a protest song that never raises its voice. Almost no processing, almost no rhythm, the 44 doing what a 44 does.

Chick a Boom

A hidden track, entirely voice. Waits considered making the whole album this way and pulled back, describing the all-vocal version as bread and water: the bare rudiments and nothing else.

On Ribot, generally

Ribot's own framing of session work is to treat the guitar as a character in a play and ask what country the song is in and what decade. When he dislikes a part he has said his instinct is to ask how he can destroy it. Howard's account of a take where Ribot's feedback appeared to be entirely out of control is that he then reproduced it exactly on the next pass.

The co-author

Brennan, who does not want the spotlight

Roughly half of Bone Machine and all of Real Gone are co-written by Kathleen Brennan. Her production credit goes from associate producer in 1992 to full co-producer in 2004, which is the least interesting way to describe what changed.

On Bone Machine

Asked which songs were hers, Waits said he could not separate them, that they were mixed up together. What is documented is the friction: she wanted less of the hellish noise layer on “Such a Scream” and he wanted more, and it was settled with playing cards. She refused to let him bin “I Don't Wanna Grow Up.” She brought the melody for “A Little Rain.”

His own metaphor for the division of labour from this period is that he is the prospector and she is the cook: he brings it home, she decides what it becomes.

On Real Gone

Waits described the split as not always even, sometimes seventy-thirty, and said she likes it backstage while he likes the spotlight. Howard, watching her work on the floor, described her function as editorial: you can say that, but move this. His view of the album is that it pushed further than it otherwise would have and contains sounds he would not have arrived at alone.

The record where he removed the piano and used his own mouth as a drum machine is also the record where her credit is largest. That is not a coincidence worth being coy about.

She pushed me out into traffic in a stroller. Waits on what a good collaborator does · collected interviews confirmed

The frame most often attributed to her is the one that reduces his entire catalogue to two species of song: the grand weepers and the grim reapers. It is a joke, and it is also an operating system. Every Waits record since 1983 is a ratio between those two, and Bone Machine and Real Gone are the two albums that push the reaper side hardest without abandoning the weepers entirely, which is why each one closes with something tender after forty minutes of assault.

Method

How a record gets decided, built and stopped

Neither album was assembled from whatever accumulated. Both were governed by rules set before anyone played, and both were stopped by a decision rather than by completion. This is the shape the process takes, drawn from what the two of them and the people around them have described.

  1. Set a constraint that removes an option

    Not a theme, a prohibition. On Bone Machine: strip everything down to percussion and voice, in a raw room, and hit things that were not designed to be hit. On Real Gone: no keyboards at all, and the voice does the job a drum kit would normally do. The constraint comes first and the songs are then obliged to survive it.

  2. Find the building

    Not the studio, the building. Waits rejected a proper tracking room, tried his own house, and settled on a concrete outbuilding. Twelve years later he went looking for somewhere with an unusual sound and no studio politics and found an empty school. The room is chosen before the arrangements are.

  3. Bring far too much and use almost none of it

    His stated logic: the instrument you leave behind is the one the song will demand. Hundreds of instruments go in. The piano went in for Real Gone and became furniture. The Conundrum went in for Bone Machine and made one track.

  4. Write in the room, fast, structurally unsound

    Three-legged chairs. Songs drummed and shouted rather than composed at an instrument that suggests harmony, then written down in a private shorthand he has described as hieroglyphics. Titles, overheard lines and newspaper stories enter as raw material at this stage, not as themes decided in advance.

  5. Keep the mistake if the mistake has better information in it

    A slamming bathroom door becomes the groove. A broken compressor stays in the chain because broken is a sound. The outdoor take wins over four indoor attempts. The test applied is not whether it is correct, it is whether it tells you where you are.

  6. Ask the other producer whether it is any good

    Waits has described handing material to Brennan with a direct question about whether it is worthless, and has been explicit that you have to be prepared for the answer to be yes. This is the editorial gate, and it happens before the mixing decisions, not after.

  7. Stop, because stopping is a choice

    His position is that nobody finishes anything, it gets taken away, and that with another thirty seconds everything might have been different. A song is not finished until it is recorded, and recording it requires medical precision and blind intuition at the same time. Sequencing, title and artwork then sit on top of a record whose identity was determined by the constraint in step one.

The comparison

Twelve years: what held, what inverted

Carried over

The building decides. A non-studio space chosen for its unusual sound, both times.

Larry Taylor and Brain. Two of the same players anchoring both rhythm approaches. Les Claypool appears on both.

Percussion before harmony. Rhythm is the writing tool in 1992 and in 2004.

Cheap, broken, overloaded. A broken compressor and a megaphone in 1992; a driven cassette preamp in 2004. Same instinct, different decade's hardware.

Kathleen Brennan. Co-writing and co-producing both.

Keep the accident. Asphalt in 1992, a bathroom door in 2004.

Inverted

Where the beat comes from. A man hitting metal, versus a man's mouth into an overloaded input.

Keyboards. Chamberlin and piano present, versus banned by outcome.

Who is central. Waits's own drumming, versus Marc Ribot's guitar as the lead instrumental voice.

Family. A son on turntables and a son on clarinet: hip-hop enters the building through the children.

The recording system. Analogue console and tape fifty yards uphill, versus a portable hard-disk rig in the same room as the band.

Where the engineer sits. Behind glass and up a hill, versus in the room with no glass at all.

The honest summary is that these are the same record made by opposite means. Bone Machine puts a microphone in front of a violent physical event and lets the concrete answer. Real Gone commits the violence to cassette first, in private, and then hires people to play around the damage. In both cases the thing being protected is the same: the sound of a specific person in a specific room on a specific night, with nothing in the chain smart enough to clean it up.
