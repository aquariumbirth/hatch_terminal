Part I

The Physics

Volume I gave you names. This volume gives you reasons. Every rule of voicing, every "that sounds muddy," every chord that feels inevitable traces back to one fact: a vibrating string does not make one pitch. It makes a whole family of them.

The chord hiding inside one note

Play the hero diagram above again and listen closely to just the first note. A low C contains, faintly and in order: the octave, the fifth, another octave, the major third, the fifth again, a flat-ish seventh, and the octave once more. That is the overtone series, and it is not theory, it is acoustics. Which means:

  • The major triad is not a human invention. It is an amplification of what a single bass note is already doing. This is why root position triads feel like gravity.
  • The fifth is the strongest non-octave overtone, which is why it sounds like structure rather than color. Part II is entirely about this.
  • A dominant 7th is baked into nature too, slightly flat. The blues knew this before the textbooks did.
Why intervals have flavors. Simple vibration ratios (2:3 for the fifth, 4:5 for the major third) line their overtones up neatly: consonance. Complex ratios collide: tension. The interval table from Volume I was really a table of ratio complexity wearing poetic names.

Register changes the interval

The same interval is a different object on a different floor of the keyboard. Down low, every note's overtones are loud and closely packed, so close intervals fight. Up high, the overtones thin out and the same interval turns transparent. Play these two minor thirds back to back:

Inversions: every interval has a twin

Flip any interval by moving its bottom note up an octave and you get its inversion. The pair always adds up to 12 half steps, and the two feel like relatives, not strangers: the aching minor 2nd inverts into the aching major 7th. Hear one pair:

Practical payoff: when a voicing feels crowded, invert an interval inside it. The harmony survives, the texture opens. That single trick generates half the voicings in Part VII.

Part II

Fifths

The fifth is the skeleton key of Western music: the strongest interval after the octave, the engine of every key relationship, and the entire harmonic language of half the film scores you love. This chapter is short on chords and long on structure.

The atom

Root plus fifth. No third, so no major, no minor, no opinion. Just architecture. On piano it is a power chord; on a synth pad it is a landscape; under a melody it is a score.

Stack them and you get weather

Keep climbing in fifths and no chord ever quite forms; instead you get a huge, keyless shimmer. This is a favorite Zimmer string pad and a beautiful two-hand piano object. Hold it with the damper pedal and let the overtones argue politely.

The alternating bass

The oldest left hand in American music: root, fifth, root, fifth, while the right hand does anything at all. Country, folk, gospel, and every Dylan song ever hammered out on an upright. It works because the fifth supports the chord without restating it, so the bass moves while the harmony stands still.

The circle of fifths · the map

Sort all twelve keys by fifths and they close into a circle. This diagram is the single most useful picture in music, for three reasons. Neighbors are family: adjacent keys share six of their seven notes, so moving one step is smooth and moving three steps is drama. The V is always one step clockwise: want the dominant of any key, look right. The relative minor is the inner ring: every major key has a minor twin using the same notes. Click any name to hear its home chord.

outer ring: major keys · inner ring: relative minors · click to hear

Use it as a composer, not a theorist: when a section feels stuck, borrow a chord from one neighbor over; when you want the floor to drop, jump across the circle. Wilson's famous key lifts, Part VI, are circle moves made with total confidence.

Fifths on your instruments. On piano, low fifths are free weight. On Rhodes, a bass fifth blooms; add the octave and it turns to fog, so usually don't. On Wurli, keep fifths mid-height or they woof. On a synth pad, a single low fifth plus one high color note is a full arrangement. More on all of this in Part IV.

Sevenths

Volume I used seventh chords. This chapter builds all of them from one root so you can watch the machinery. Five of the seven flavors are one half step apart from each other, which means the whole vocabulary is closer than it looks.

The tritone engine

Why does a dominant 7th demand resolution? Because its 3rd and 7th form a tritone, and a tritone is unstable by ratio. In G7 those notes are B and F; each is one half step from a note of C major, so the pair collapses inward like a held breath released. Every V to I cadence in existence is this one spring uncoiling.

Dominant chains · the circle in motion

Any dominant can resolve to another dominant a fifth below, which can resolve again, and again: a chain of springs, each release loading the next. This is the circle of fifths as an engine instead of a map, and it is the sound of a bridge finding its way home. Notice the right hand: the 3rd and 7th simply trade places at every step.

E7 → A7 → D7 → G7 → C

The gospel resolve

Suspend the dominant's 3rd, then let it arrive late. That one delayed half step is most of gospel piano, most of Sunday, and a move Cave and Medeski both live on. Play it slowly enough to feel the arrival.

Part IV

Hands & Instruments

Technique is not athletics, it is ergonomics: getting sound out with the least effort and the most control. And the same hands make different decisions on piano, Rhodes, Wurli, and a synth patch. Both halves of that sentence live in this chapter.

The setup, once and for all

  • Bench height: forearms level with the keys or a hair above, elbows just forward of your torso. If your wrists dip below the keybed, raise the seat, not your shoulders.
  • Distance: far enough that your elbows never touch your ribs when you cross the middle of the keyboard.
  • Default hand: a loose curve, as if holding a small apple. Fingertips contact the keys; the thumb plays on its side edge. Flat fingers are not wrong, they are a color: warmer, quieter, more felt-like. Frahm practically lives there.
  • The sound comes from arm weight, not finger strength. Loud is letting more arm fall in; soft is holding more arm back. Fingers aim, arms deliver. The moment your forearm burns, you are pressing, not playing.
  • The tension ritual: at the end of every phrase, let the shoulders drop. Make it a habit now and every hard thing later gets easier.

Home position and the two grips

The five-finger position is the hand's home: one finger per key, no stretching, thumb on the root for the right hand and pinky on the root for the left. Nearly everything in both books is this shape, slightly opened. When you must move, relocate the whole hand rather than stretching fingers from a fixed wrist: float, land, settle. The keyboard is a series of homes, not one home with long hallways.

Spans: if a 10th does not fit your hand, roll it, pinky first, and pedal the bass note. A rolled 10th played with confidence sounds better than a strained one played on time. Octave passages bounce from a loose wrist, like dribbling a very small basketball; the forearm stays quiet or you will feel it tomorrow.

Same hands, four instruments

You play piano, Rhodes, Wurli, and synth plugins. A voicing is not portable between them without adjustment, because each instrument has a different overtone story. The differences that matter:

InstrumentWhat changesVoicing advice
PianoRich lows, singing top, long decayEverything in both books as written. The reference instrument.
RhodesBell overtones, weak fundamentals, the famous bark when struck hard around the middleVoice tighter and higher than piano. Low 3rds are mud squared: keep them above C3 and a half. Fourths and clusters sound gorgeous. Fewer notes, more space.
WurliReedy midrange, less sustain, breaks up sweetly when pushedBuilt for staccato stabs and gospel comping. Keep voicings mid-height, 3 to 4 notes. The distortion at high velocity is a feature: that is the Wurli saying yes.
Synth padsEvery note adds oscillators; density gets expensive fastTwo or three notes maximum. Root and fifth low, one color note high, and let detune and filter provide the richness a pianist would add with fingers.
The one-question test. Before copying a piano voicing to another patch, ask: what is this instrument already providing? Rhodes provides shimmer, so remove the notes that add shimmer. A pad provides sustain and width, so remove almost everything. The instrument is a band member, not a canvas.
Part V

The Three Pedals

Your Nord's triple pedal gives you the full grand piano set: damper on the right, sostenuto in the middle, una corda on the left. Most players use one of the three, badly. Learn all three and you gain a second pair of hands.

Right pedal · the damper

Pressing it lifts every damper off every string, so notes ring after your fingers leave and every string sympathetically hums along. Two truths follow. First, it is a connector: the only way to join chords your hands cannot physically link. Second, it is an accumulator: everything you play stacks until you clear it, which is either haze or sludge depending on intent.

The core skill is legato pedaling, and the timing is the whole trick: the foot comes up exactly as the new chord lands, and goes back down just after. Up on the change, down after the change. Your hands connect nothing; the pedal does it all.

Legato pedaling: the pedal releases on each new chord and re-catches it a moment later. The overlap in your hands is zero; the overlap in the room is seamless.

Half pedal: your Nord's damper is continuous, not a switch. Halfway down, the dampers brush the strings: notes ring but decay faster, giving haze without flood. This is the Badalamenti fog and the ballad player's secret. Practice holding a chord and slowly raising the pedal from full to half to off, listening to the room change. And regularly practice with no pedal, so your fingers own legato and the pedal becomes a choice instead of a crutch.

Middle pedal · sostenuto, the specialist

Sostenuto sustains only the notes already down when you press it, and nothing you play afterward. That makes it a third hand: catch a low note or fifth, and then play bone-dry above it. Press play below and you will hear the trick: the left hand fifth hangs in the air while the right hand stabs stay short.

The recipe, step by step: play the low fifth, press the middle pedal while holding it, release your hand, and it keeps ringing. Now both hands are free and the right pedal is still available on top for phrasing. Drone plus dry ostinato is an entire genre, and this pedal is how one human plays it.

Left pedal · una corda, the color

On a grand, the action shifts so hammers strike fewer strings on a softer part of the felt; the sound is not just quieter, it is rounder, with the attack sanded off. The pedals on your rig

Some practical Nord-and-plugin notes. On acoustic piano programs the triple pedal gives you continuous half-damper response, plus the sampled thump and string-wash of the pedal mechanism itself, which sells realism more than people expect; try playing a bar of nothing but pedal noise and you will hear it. On Rhodes and Wurli patches the damper behaves like the real instruments' sustain, but real EPs never had sostenuto or una corda, so how the other two pedals act depends on the program: check each favorite patch for a minute rather than assuming. In your DAW, the three pedals speak CC64 (damper), CC66 (sostenuto), and CC67 (soft); good piano plugins respond to continuous CC64 for half pedaling, and many synth plugins map CC64 to envelope release, which means "sustain" on a pad is really "longer goodbye." For pads, consider skipping the pedal entirely and lengthening the release instead.

A one-week pedal curriculum. Two days on legato pedaling with any two chords from Part III. Two days on the sostenuto trick with the diagram above, until the catch is casual. Two days playing a favorite piece entirely under una corda, listening for what the softness does to your time feel. Day seven: all three in one improvisation, because that is the actual instrument.
Part VI

The Artists

Devices again, not transcriptions: the repeatable moves under each writer's sound, playable in miniature. One study runs deeper than the rest, because you asked for it and because he earned it.

The deep study · the bass is a melody

Brian Wilson

Wilson's revolution, the one inside God Only Knows and Good Vibrations, is deciding that the bass note is a voice with its own opinion, not the servant of the chord above it. God Only Knows famously opens away from its home key and spends the song on inversions, so the bass sings a stepwise line while the harmony changes overhead; you are often not sure what key you are in, only that you are being carried. The device, distilled: write the bassline first, make it stepwise, then choose chords that happen to contain those bass notes. Good Vibrations shows the other Wilson machine: a minor verse built on a stepwise descending progression, and then, instead of developing it, he lifts entire sections into new keys, assembling the song from modules like a producer, which is exactly what he was. The descent below is the classic shape; play it, then play the whole thing again starting one whole step higher and feel that Wilson section-lift do its work.

The elevator down · then lift the building

Three more Wilson habits worth stealing outright: added 6ths and 9ths on major chords instead of plain triads, warmth without jazz flavor (the Vault's Pet Sound chord); root motion by thirds, sliding to chords a third away for that weightless drift; and ending phrases on inversions instead of roots, so sections feel handed off rather than closed. His arrangements did the rest with instruments, but every one of these decisions fits under two hands.

The pulpit at midnight

Nick Cave

Cave's piano is gospel slowed to a confession: root position triads played with full arm weight, left hand octaves, tempos where every chord is an event. The sophistication is in the restraint; the harmony stays plain so the voice can bleed. Steal the posture more than the notes: sit down, play the triad below in slow 6/8, repeat it until it means something, and resist every urge to decorate.

The three-chord cathedral

Bob Dylan

Dylan at the piano is I, IV, and V treated as a complete universe, usually with the alternating bass from Part II underneath. The device worth studying is the gospel walkup: reaching IV by stepping the bass through the tonic's own inversion, so three chords feel like a procession. Words lead, harmony carries, nobody shows off. It is harder than it sounds, which is the entire Dylan lesson.

The two-voice mind

Brad Mehldau

Mehldau's core move is refusing to let the left hand be a chord machine. It plays lines: countermelodies, walking inner voices, a second singer. Start with the smallest version: hold one right hand chord and let the left hand walk a scale underneath it. The chord reharmonizes itself with every bass note, free of charge. His other trademarks, odd meters and pop tunes treated as standards, both grow from this same two-voice independence. Practice the strip below until the left hand feels like melody, then let it wander further.

The felt room

Nils Frahm

Frahm's grammar: una corda down for entire pieces, close mid-register cells looped past the point of hypnosis, and composition by changing one note at a time. The two figures below differ by a single pitch; that single pitch is the event, and at his tempos it lands like a scene change. Add the sostenuto drone trick from Part V under either one and you have the full architecture: held bass, soft loop, patience.

The string section of one

Jonny Greenwood

Greenwood scores treat the piano as a string ensemble: white-key clusters played as a single object, like a section bowing adjacent notes; modal ostinatos that never cadence; sudden registral leaps from basement to attic. The cluster below is neither a chord nor a mistake, it is a texture with a root. Hold it under the damper pedal, then lift into a clean triad and hear the fog become a face.

The decay artists

Reznor & Ross

The score-era Reznor and Atticus Ross toolkit, distinct from the Volume I songbook: drones with events above them. A held interval establishes the room; sparse, dissonant dyads arrive high and far away; then everything is allowed to die naturally, because the decay is the note. The sostenuto pedal was invented for this a century early. Press play and count how much silence they would leave between the stabs. Then leave more.

The pulse

John Carpenter

Carpenter proves harmony is optional if the pulse is right: octave eighth notes that never blink, one minor chord as the entire universe, and then, when your guard drops, a single move to bVI, the coldest change in minor. His famous main themes add rhythmic unease by dropping or adding a beat, so the loop never quite lets you settle. On your rig this is a synth-bass patch, the ostinato below, and nerve.

The monolith builder

Hans Zimmer

Zimmer's harmonic language is deliberately small so the sound design can be enormous: stacked fifths, sus chords that never declare major or minor, four-chord loops with one common tone threading them, and melody moving at a tenth the speed of the pulse beneath it. For a keyboardist the lesson is orchestration: the stack below on a huge pad, one slow top line, and restraint measured in minutes. Simple is not the compromise; simple is the delivery system.

The return

Angelo Badalamenti

Volume I covered his shadows: the line cliché, harmonic minor, the m(maj7). Here is his other half, the dream: wide major add9 voicings, a slow left hand tremolo between root and fifth, half pedal fog, and tempos on the edge of stopping. Menace and reverie were never different techniques for him, only different chords played with the same patience.
