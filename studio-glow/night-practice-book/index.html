Part I

The Why

You already hear all of this. Playing by ear means your ear did the theory without telling you. This chapter just attaches names to things you can already recognize, so you can find them on purpose.

Everything is distance

Harmony has one raw material: the interval, the distance between two notes, counted in half steps (one key to the very next key, black or white). Every chord quality, every scale color, every mood you associate with a record is a particular set of distances. Memorize this table slowly over a couple of weeks. It is the periodic table for the rest of the book.

Chords are stacked thirds

Take any scale, grab a note, skip one, grab, skip, grab. That gives you a triad: root, 3rd, 5th. The quality of a triad comes entirely from which thirds you stacked:

  • Major = major 3rd, then minor 3rd on top (4 + 3 half steps). C · E · G
  • Minor = minor 3rd, then major 3rd (3 + 4). A · C · E
  • Diminished = two minor 3rds (3 + 3). Compressed, anxious. B · D · F
  • Augmented = two major 3rds (4 + 4). Weightless, wrong in a useful way. C · E · G#

Keep stacking thirds and you get seventh chords, where most of the music you love actually lives:

SymbolRecipeSound
Cmaj7major triad + major 7thDreamy, suspended in amber. Lytle's home base.

Cm7minor triad + minor 7thSmoky, settled minor. The default jazz minor chord.
Cm(maj7)minor triad + major 7thNoir. Beautiful and wrong at once. Twin Peaks in one chord.
Cm7b5diminished triad + minor 7thHalf-diminished. The saddest setup chord in music.

Extensions and the decoder ring

Keep stacking past the 7th and you reach 9, 11, and 13. These are just scale steps 2, 4, and 6 played an octave up, where they turn from clutter into color. Two conventions worth knowing: C9 implies the 7th is in there too, while Cadd9 means a plain triad with a 9 added and no 7th. That second one is the Reznor and Lytle chord. And a slash chord like G/B just means a G chord with B as the lowest note. That is how songwriters make basslines walk.

The ear-first shortcut. When a chord in a record hits you, stop asking "what chord is that" and ask "what distances am I hearing." A minor 3rd somewhere? A major 7th rubbing the root? That question is answerable by ear, and the answer is the chord symbol. Theory is not a different skill from your ear. It is your ear, with a filing system.
Part II

Minor, Five Ways

Minor is not one scale. It is a neighborhood of scales that share a flat 3rd and disagree about everything else. Each disagreement is a different shade of dark, and each of your artists has a favorite street.

All the diagrams below are in A so you can see the shapes clearly, mostly on white keys. The circled numbers are right hand fingering. Left hand fingering for every seven-note scale here is the mirror pattern: 5 4 3 2 1 3 2 1 going up.

Natural minor · the default dark

Flat 3, flat 6, flat 7. This is the minor your ear grew up on: most rock, most folk, most of what Reznor and Yorke write sits here (its formal name is the Aeolian mode). Its signature move is the bVII to i cadence, G to Am, which resolves without any leading-tone drama.

Harmonic minor · the noir lift

Raise the 7th of natural minor and two things happen. You gain a leading tone (G# pulls hard back to A), and you create a haunted gap of three half steps between F and G#. That gap is the "exotic" sound in film scores, in Tori's dramatic moments, and all over Badalamenti. This scale is also the factory that produces Am(maj7) and the menacing E7b9. One altered note, an entire genre.

Melodic minor · the jazz minor

Raise the 6th as well and the top half of the scale turns bright while the bottom stays dark. Jazz players use this form going up and down and just call it the jazz minor. It is Bill Evans country: the source of m6, m(maj9), and the lush minor voicings later in this book. If harmonic minor is a black-and-white film, melodic minor is the same film in color.

Dorian · the cool minor

Natural minor with a raised 6th and the b7 kept. Minor, but with the lights on: melancholy that can still groove. Modal jazz is built on it, and a lot of Radiohead's "minor but strangely hopeful" passages are Dorian in disguise. Quick ear test: if the minor song sounds like it could keep going forever without needing to resolve, suspect Dorian.

Phrygian · the threat

Natural minor with a flat 2. That b2 leaning on the root is the darkest color in the modal system: flamenco, metal, industrial dread. Play the first two notes alone, A to Bb, and you already have the mood. Reznor's most claustrophobic piano moments live on this half step.

The blues scale · the barroom

Six notes: minor pentatonic plus the flat 5. Not a "scale" in the choir sense, more a set of licensed dirty notes. This is the Tom Waits floor plan, and it works over major chords, minor chords, and chords that have given up entirely.

And one door into the light

For Lytle's and Bill Fay's warmer material you need plain major too. Same fingering logic, thumb on C. When Lytle's chords shimmer more than plain major should, he is usually borrowing Lydian color: a major scale with a raised 4th (in C, an F#). File that away for the maj7#11 voicing in the Vault.

How fingering actually works

Every scale fingering in existence follows two rules: the thumb stays off black keys whenever possible, and the hand travels in alternating groups of three and four with the thumb tucking under after finger 3. Do not memorize fingerings as number strings. Feel them as two hand shapes, a triangle and a square, walking up the keyboard. Practice slowly enough that the thumb tuck is silent and invisible, because speed is just a relaxed tuck repeated.

ScaleWho lives there
Natural minorReznor, Yorke, Tori, most songs you already play

Practice these as sounds, not exercises. One octave, hands separate, slow, and after each pass improvise for thirty seconds using only that scale over a droning open fifth in the left hand (A and E). The drone turns a fingering drill into an atmosphere, and you will learn the scale's personality along with its shape. That is the whole point.
Part III

Voicings

A chord is a decision about which notes to include. A voicing is a decision about where to put them. Same chord symbol, different voicing, completely different record. This is the chapter that changes how you sound.

First, the problem

Here is how most self-taught players voice A minor: everything crammed into one fist, low on the keyboard. Play it and listen honestly.

The fix is a physics rule called the low interval limit: close intervals blur together in the bass register. Rough guide: keep 3rds above C3, keep 2nds above E3, and below C2 play only single roots, octaves, and fifths. Bass wants space; treble tolerates crowds. Nearly every voicing that follows is just this rule applied with taste.

The spread triad

Same three notes as the mud, redistributed. Root and fifth low and open in the left hand, the sensitive third up where it can actually speak. This single change is eighty percent of sounding "professional."

Shells · the minimum viable chord

Jazz players discovered you can delete most of a chord. A shell is the root plus one defining interval, usually the 7th, sometimes the 3rd stretched to a 10th. Two notes that imply everything. Shells leave enormous space for a voice or a right hand melody, which is why sparse writers from Evans to Reznor lean on them.

Guide tones · why progressions feel inevitable

Inside every 7th chord, the 3rd and 7th do all the talking. They define the quality, and in a good progression they resolve into each other by half steps while the roots jump around underneath. Play this ii V I in C and watch the right hand barely move. That barely-moving is called voice leading, and it is the entire secret of "smooth."

ii · V · I in C major

Rootless voicings · the Bill Evans sound

Evans' great trick: since the bass player has the root, the piano can spend its fingers on color instead. A rootless voicing keeps the 3rd and 7th and adds the 9 or 13. Playing solo, you supply the root yourself with a light left hand. The result is that floating, expensive sound that makes a plain progression feel like rain on a window. These three shapes, moved through keys, are a lifetime supply.

The same ii · V · I, rootless

The minor ii · V · i · the crown jewel

Now the dark version, the progression under half the ballads ever written and most of the noir canon. Watch the top of the right hand: A falls to G#, then the whole shape settles onto the m(maj7). Every note that moves, moves by half step. Learn this in A minor until it plays itself, then move it to E minor and D minor.

Add9 and sus · modern melancholy

The add9 is the defining color of post-1990 minor piano. In the version below, the 9 (B) sits directly against the b3 (C), a deliberate one-half-step rub that reads as ache rather than error. The sus2 goes the other way: remove the third entirely and the chord becomes weather, neither happy nor sad. Yorke writes whole passages of sus chords that never resolve, which is exactly why they feel suspended in air.

Quartal voicings · stacked fourths

Abandon thirds entirely and stack perfect 4ths. The result is ambiguous, open, modern: it implies a mode rather than declaring a chord. This exact shape, four 4ths capped with a 3rd, is the most famous piano voicing in jazz, and it sounds fully at home under a Radiohead-style melody. Try it over a low D drone.

Inversions and slash chords · the walking bass

Any chord can put its 3rd or 5th in the bass. Songwriters use inversions to make the bassline step where the chords leap. Here the harmony goes C, G, Am, but the bass walks calmly down C, B, A. This device is everywhere in Fay and Lytle, and once you hear it you cannot unhear it.

C · G/B · Am

The diminished trapdoor

A dim7 chord is four minor 3rds, perfectly symmetrical, belonging to no key. Classic use: slip it between two diatonic chords a whole step apart as a chromatic staircase, C to C#dim7 to Dm7. Gospel, stride, and Tom Waits all run on this move.

How to practice any voicing. Three steps, always. One: loop it until your hand finds it without looking. Two: transpose it to two other keys, because a voicing you know in one key is a photograph and a voicing you know in three keys is a tool. Three: put one melody note on top and keep the voicing underneath it. Step three is where voicings become music.
Part IV

Two Hands, Two Jobs

The piano is a duo pretending to be one instrument. The left hand is the rhythm section: weight, time, foundation. The right hand is the singer: melody, color, phrasing. Most "I can't play with both hands" problems are really "both hands are trying to do the same job."

The left hand menu

Build your left hand from this menu, simplest first. Each one is a complete accompaniment by itself.

The right hand's one rule

The top note is the melody, whether you meant it or not. Ears track the highest sounding pitch. So when you play chords, choose the top note like a singer chooses a note, and rearrange the chord underneath it. This is why two voicings of the same chord tell different stories, and it is the actual meaning of Evans' famous lyricism: he sang with the top of his voicings. A beautiful exercise: play any progression from this book and force the top notes to make a melody that steps rather than leaps.

The other great right hand role is the broken figure: arpeggiating a voicing instead of blocking it. Tori builds entire songs from a rolling add9 pattern that never states the chord all at once. Here is one to loop until it disappears into muscle:

Putting the hands together

  • Hands separate first, always. Loop each hand until it is boring. Boredom is the signal that the hand has automated and freed your attention.
  • Combine at half speed. The tempo where both hands work together cleanly is your real tempo. Speed is a byproduct of relaxation, never of effort.
  • Anchor on downbeats. When coordinating a new pattern, make the hands meet on beat 1 and let the rest be approximate at first. Precision spreads outward from the downbeat.
  • Give the hands different densities. Busy left hand, sustained right hand. Or the reverse. Two busy hands is a traffic jam.

The pedal, briefly

The sustain pedal is not glue for your mistakes, it is a lighting rig. Three habits cover ninety percent of use: change the pedal just after each new chord lands (called legato pedaling, the down-up happens as the fingers strike), keep it shallower in the bass register where mud accumulates, and periodically play with no pedal at all so your fingers, not your foot, learn to connect notes. For the Badalamenti tremolo-and-haze sound, half pedal plus a slow left hand is the entire recipe.

Part V

The Artists

Not transcriptions. Devices. Each of these writers owns a small set of harmonic moves, and once you can play the move, you can hear it everywhere in their catalog and use it in yours.

The slow dread

Angelo Badalamenti

The Twin Peaks sound is harmonic minor played at half speed with the pedal down. Its centerpiece is the line cliché: hold a minor chord and walk one inner voice down by half steps, 1 to 7 to b7 to 6. Four chords, one moving finger, infinite menace. Play it slowly, let each chord bloom fully, and add a soft left hand tremolo between root and fifth when you want the fog to roll in.

The line cliché in A minor
The lyricist of voicings

Bill Evans

Evans' whole aesthetic reduces to three habits: rootless voicings (Part III, learn them cold), the top note sings (choose it like a melody, always), and inner voices move. That last one is subtle and devastating: while the chord appears to sit still, one interior note steps somewhere and the harmony breathes. Here is the smallest possible example. Play it and notice you heard a melody even though "nothing happened."

Inner motion

His other lesson is restraint under a melody: shells and guide tones low, air in the middle, tune on top. When in doubt, remove a note.

The machine with a heart

Trent Reznor

Reznor's piano writing is minimal by force of will: a left hand ostinato that never varies, an add9 ache in the right hand, and the Phrygian b2 when he wants your skin to crawl. The restraint is the point. One figure loops while a single chord change lands like an event, because in an empty room every object is enormous. Loop the ostinato from Part IV, then try the voicing below over it and hold it for four slow bars.

The beautiful wrong turn

Thom Yorke

Radiohead harmony runs on three devices. First, oscillation: rock between two chords that share most of their notes and let the bass tell the story. Below, the right hand literally does not move while the harmony transforms. Second, modal mixture: in a major key, borrow the minor iv (F to Fm in the key of C) for that instant heartbreak dip. Third, sus chords that never resolve, so the music hangs in the air permanently. All three are cheap to play and expensive-sounding, the best kind of device.

Oscillation · same hand, new world
The rolling engine

Tori Amos

Tori's signature is motion as harmony: the broken add9 figure from Part IV, driven hard, often over insistent left hand octaves in straight quarters. The chords are frequently simple; the ceaseless arpeggiation is what makes them sound orchestral. Her second signature is reaching for harmonic minor at emotional peaks, that raised 7th cutting through like a struck match. Practice recipe: loop the broken figure, move the left hand bass through A, F, C, G underneath it without stopping the right hand. That independence is the entire trick.

The haunted saloon

Tom Waits

Waits plays traditional forms through a broken speaker: stride-lite left hand, 6th chords for the sweet lie, the diminished trapdoor between chords, the blues scale smeared over everything, and when the song turns sinister, a naked tritone in the low register. The growl below is the Hendrix chord relocated to a piano bar: a dominant with a #9, major and minor third fighting in public.

The hymnal

Bill Fay

Fay's harmony is gospel slowed to walking pace: plagal motion (IV to I, the "amen" cadence) instead of dominant drama, often over a pedal bass that refuses to move, plus suspensions that resolve gently downward. Hymn spacing matters here: left hand root and fifth, right hand close triad an octave up, nothing crowded. It sounds like forgiveness, which is the point.

The amen over a pedal
The transmission from the desert

Jason Lytle

Grandaddy's keyboard language is major 7 shimmer over pedal points: the bass holds one note while triads drift overhead, so even ordinary chords feel like weather systems passing a fixed observer. Add 9ths freely, play in the upper half of the keyboard, and let the pedal blur adjacent chords slightly. Melancholy optimism is just Cmaj7 refusing to leave C in the bass.

Part VII

The Six Week Plan

Twenty to thirty minutes a day beats three hours on Sunday, every time. Each week has one idea. Resist the urge to rush ahead: the point is depth, not coverage, and the book will still be here.

Week 1

Names for what you hear

Daily: the interval table, played and sung, five minutes. A natural minor, hands separate, one octave. Then the drone game from Part II: open fifth in the left hand, improvise in the scale with the right. End each session by playing one chord from a song you know and naming its quality out loud.

Week 2

Shells and guide tones

Shell voicings for Am7, Dm7, E7. The major ii V I with guide tones in C, then G, then F. The real assignment: take one song you already play by ear and replace every chord with a shell. Feel how much room appears.

Week 3

The noir kit

A harmonic minor daily. The line cliché until it is automatic. The minor ii V i in A minor, slow, with pedal. By Friday, improvise a slow melody over the line cliché with the right hand. Congratulations, you are scoring an imaginary film.

Week 4

The Evans week

Rootless major ii V I in C, F, and G. Rootless minor ii V i in A minor and E minor. Practice singing the top note of each voicing as you play it. This is the week your recordings start sounding like records.

Week 5

Left hand independence

The ostinato and open-fifth patterns under a free right hand. Stride-lite through a slow 12-bar blues in A. Tori drill: keep the broken add9 figure running while the left hand moves through A, F, C, G. Sloppy is fine; continuous is mandatory.

Week 6

Theft

Choose five Vault voicings and write an eight-bar progression that uses all of them. Then reharmonize one of your existing songs: swap plain triads for add9s, maj7s, and one well-placed m(maj7). Record both versions. Compare. Keep what serves the song.

The ear-to-theory bridge

You learned by ear, which means your ear is the senior partner. These four exercises make theory report to it, a few minutes each, forever:

  • Quality flash. Play a random triad or 7th chord, eyes closed on the name. Call major, minor, dominant, or dim before your brain can count. Wrong answers are data, not failures.
  • Find the seventh. Play any triad, then hunt by ear for the note a whole step below the octave (the b7) and a half step below (the maj7). Feel the difference in your chest, not your head.
  • Degree singing. Play a slow A minor scale and sing the degree numbers. Then play a chord from this book and sing its top note's degree. This is how ear players become arrangers.
  • One chord a day. Take one chord from a record you love and find it on the keys. Just one. Name it with Part I's decoder. In a year that is three hundred chords that are actually yours.
A last word. Theory is descriptive, not prescriptive. Every rule in this book is a summary of what already sounded good to someone's ear, including yours. When a voicing in here collides with an instinct of yours, trust the instinct and then figure out what the instinct knew. That second step is the whole education.
