00 / Summary

Three things decide how far this instrument goes

The first-generation Nord Piano is a well-built, superb-sounding stage piano with three specific ceilings: a two-sensor keybed, a sample memory frozen at library v5.x, and a triple pedal that talks to its own engine far better than it talks to yours. Every worthwhile upgrade path runs through one of those three.

The short version

  • Use it as a controller. The single biggest jump in usefulness is running it into a plugin host. Pianoteq is the standout partner because physical modeling handles repedaling, half-pedal, sympathetic resonance, and Rhodes bark as continuous behavior rather than crossfaded snapshots.
  • Fix the pedal before you fix anything else. The generation-one Nord Triple Pedal sends stepped CC64, not a smooth sweep. A separate continuous sustain pedal into a USB MIDI pedal interface gives you real half-pedaling in plugins for well under a hundred dollars.
  • For instant-on Rhodes, the answer is a Raspberry Pi. Pianoteq runs natively on ARM Linux with no samples to stream. Documented builds boot to a loaded patch in roughly 23 seconds at around 1.33 ms of latency. Your Lenovo remains the right tool for anything Kontakt-based.
  • The retrigger problem is hardware. Software can mask it, script around its edges, and stop plugins choking on it. Nothing can make the keybed emit a note-on it never generated.
500 MB Total sample memory on the first-generation Nord Piano. Enough for a handful of large grands plus the electrics. The Grand Lady D alone is 78.6 MB with full resonance samples.

What is genuinely fixable

Hard limit: fast repetition Hard limit: no aftertouch Hard limit: library v6 sounds Partial: pedal resolution Partial: organ ergonomics Solved: piano and EP realism Solved: instant-on operation Solved: sliders and drawbars

Those three hard limits are worth internalising early, because a lot of forum advice quietly promises to solve them and cannot. Everything else on that list responds well to a few hundred dollars and an afternoon of setup.

01 / The instrument

Build, keybed, and where it ranks in 2026

Model history and firmware

Clavia launched the Nord Piano in 2010; Sound On Sound reviewed it that August, and Nord's legacy pages put production at 2010 to 2012, when the Nord Piano 2 replaced it. It shares the sampled piano library of the Electro 3 and Stage EX of the same era.

The final OS is v1.50, released 2019-03-11, documented by Nord as a spare-part compatibility update. The last functional changes were earlier: v1.48 (2015-08-13) improved USB MIDI reliability, v1.46 (2012-11-21) fixed a pedaling performance issue, and v1.30 (2011-06-16) made the sustain pedal send CC64 value 0 when fully lifted, which the 2010 firmware did not do.

Do this first

Open Nord Sound Manager and confirm you are on OS 1.50. The v1.48 USB MIDI reliability fix and the v1.30 CC64 zero-on-release fix both matter directly to a plugin rig. If the unit has sat unused since 2013, it may still be on an OS that never sends a clean pedal-up message.

Build quality

Metal panelling, real wooden end cheeks, hand assembled. Sound On Sound recorded the dimensions as 1284 mm long, 340 mm deep, 121 mm high, at 18 kg. The rear panel gives you 5-pin MIDI in and out, USB type B for MIDI, sound transfer and firmware, one quarter-inch piano pedal input, stereo quarter-inch outputs, headphones, and a mini-jack monitor input.

What it does not give you: no pitch bend, no modulation wheel, and no expression or volume pedal input. For synth and organ work that omission bites harder than the keybed does, and it is the reason section 08 exists.

The keybed

A Fatar TP/40 hammer action, modified by Nord with a slightly deeper trigger point than the Stage EX of the same period. Two sensors per key, no escapement, untextured keytops, no aftertouch. Sound On Sound described it as just on the weighty side of average, fast and smooth, with three selectable velocity curves, all of them lighter than default. There is no heavier curve and no fixed-velocity option.

Triple-sensor Virtual Hammer Action Technology arrived on the Nord Piano 3 in 2015. There is no firmware path that adds a sensor to hardware that does not have one.

Reliability after fifteen years

The action is the weak link on every long-lived Fatar-equipped board. Expect, eventually, one of: an intermittent dead key, a single key that always plays at full velocity, or a double trigger. The usual cause is debris in the rubber-dome carbon contacts, or a failing upper sensor on a two-sensor bed, which is exactly what produces the always-loud key. Contacts clean up well. Parts and service still exist in 2026; Equipboard's listing notes key or screen repairs running in the 200 to 300 dollar range. Use a dust cover and you will likely never see any of it.

Ranking it honestly

As a pure MIDI controller in 2026 it is outclassed. The Kawai VPC1 exists specifically to drive piano plugins, with a wooden triple-sensor RM3 Grand action. The Studiologic SL88 Grand and Numa X Piano give you TP/40 Wood with three sensors and aftertouch. The Roland A-88MKII and current Nord and Kawai grand actions all read as more refined instruments.

Where the Nord still wins: the onboard sounds, the no-menu workflow, an unusually good pedal-noise and string-resonance implementation for its generation, and 18 kg. It is a better instrument than most of the controllers that beat it as a controller. That distinction shapes the whole plan: keep playing the Nord sounds, and add a plugin layer for what the Nord cannot reach.

02 / Two sensors

The retrigger problem, and what software can honestly do

You described it precisely: press down, get a note, let up slightly, press again, get nothing until the key comes all the way back. That is not a fault. That is a two-contact keybed working as designed.

What is actually happening

The Nord derives velocity by timing the interval between two contacts closing as the key travels down. Because there are only two, the electronics can only register a fresh note-on once the key has risen far enough for the upper contact to open and reset. In practice that means near-full return. Trills, repeated notes at speed, and half-key retriggering all fail at the same point.

A third sensor sits higher in the travel. The note-on still fires from the lower contact, so velocity is unchanged, but the extra sensor lets the board detect that the key has risen just enough to re-arm without a full return. As a Piano World technical thread put it: with three sensors you can retrigger faster because the key does not have to be released as high, and releasing halfway does not shut the note off. Three sensors are how a digital action models the double escapement of a grand.

Drag the key in the simulator above and toggle between the two modes. The re-arm line is the entire story.

Cannot be fixed in software

No MIDI tool can invent a note-on the hardware never generated. Any advice that claims otherwise is describing a workaround for a different problem. That said, four things genuinely help.

1. Pedal-down sustain masks it

With CC64 held, the note keeps ringing regardless of key return, so repeated notes overlap and the gap stops being audible. For ballad and pad playing this alone removes most of the complaint.

2. Plugin retrigger and release behaviour

In Pianoteq the physical model means a retriggered note blends into a string that is still resonating rather than hard-cutting a sample voice, which softens the artifact considerably. In Kontakt libraries, Noire, Alicia's Keys, and the Scarbee electrics all expose release and retrigger scripting. Reducing hard voice-cut on note-off is the setting to look for.

3. MIDI note-off scripting

Bome MIDI Translator Pro, a Reaper JSFX, Max/MSP, or a Kontakt script can inject an artificial early note-off so the plugin re-arms sooner. Working retrigger scripts circulate on the Bome and VI-Control forums; they store the last note and send a note-off ahead of the next note-on. This stops plugins behaving in an over-legato or stuck way. It does not restore fast repetition, because the missing event is upstream of the script.

4. Velocity curve work

Curve tuning does not touch the retrigger physics, but it does mean you stop compensating for the keybed by hammering it, which is where a lot of perceived unresponsiveness actually comes from.

What the Nord does send

The first-generation manual's MIDI implementation chart, dated 2010-04-02, confirms the instrument transmits and receives note-on velocity 1 to 127 and release velocity, which is more than many boards of its era offer and is usable by better piano plugins. Sustain goes out as CC64, sostenuto as CC66, soft and una corda as CC67. Aftertouch is absent in both directions.

The catch is resolution, not presence. See section 07.

03 / Nord library

What the onboard sounds can and cannot become

Memory and format

Nord's specification page gives the first-generation Nord Piano approximately 500 MB dedicated to the Nord Piano Library. It shipped with library v5 on DVD-ROM and is managed over USB with the free Nord Sound Manager, at roughly two minutes for a 50 MB piano. Polyphony is 40 voices for stereo pianos, 60 for mono.

Sizes matter at 500 MB. The flagship Grand Lady D is 39.2 MB without resonance samples and 78.6 MB with the full complement. Nord also publishes medium versions that restrict resonance to the mid range, saving 10 to 12 MB each. You can comfortably hold a few large grands plus the full electric piano set, not much more.

v5.x The ceiling. Nord Piano Library v6 content, including the White Grand and the Enhanced Electric Pianos, is not compatible with the first-generation Nord Piano. Nord's own v6 compatibility list begins at Nord Piano 3.

The compatibility wall

For years every Nord could load the newest samples. That ended with library v6. Newer instruments transcode older nsmp files forward through Sound Manager, but there is no path backward, so a first-generation unit cannot receive the v6 White Grand or the Dyno-filtered Enhanced Rhodes. This is worth knowing before you spend an evening hunting for a download that will not install.

Everything in the v5 era remains available to you, and it is a deep catalogue.

Which onboard sounds still earn their space

The electrics are the reason to keep playing this thing directly. Nord's own library notes call out the range: the 1967 Sparkletop with felt hammers, the Mk I family, the Mk II, a custom modified Mk I Suitcase, the rare Mk V, the Wurlitzer A200, the CP80, and the Clavinet D6 across four pickup positions. The Mk I is genuinely flexible, bell-like when played softly and biting when pushed.

Among acoustics, the uprights are more useful than the grands for most pop and rock work: the Petrof, the Bosendorfer, the Schimmel, and the detuned Baumgardt saloon piano all have character the grands deliberately lack. String resonance and pedal noise on this generation were, per Sound On Sound, in a different league to the shimmer of contemporary Japanese stage pianos. Note that this is the earlier String Resonance implementation with selectable pedal noise, not the Advanced String Resonance and Dynamic Pedal Noise of the Nord Piano 3 onward.

Where plugins clearly win

Deeper velocity layering, true continuous half-pedal, repedaling, una corda modeling, unlimited library size, and for Rhodes and Wurlitzer, modeled bark and tine behaviour that responds continuously rather than in steps.

Where the Nord still wins

Instant recall, no computer, no latency, no driver, and characterful electrics and uprights that sit in a mix without a single edit. Do not throw that away chasing realism you may not need on every take.

04 / Acoustic plugins

Detail you asked for: pedal release, resonance, uprights

The specific things you described, the sound when you let off the pedal, the harmonics that bloom under a held chord, repedaling into a decaying note, are exactly the things samples struggle with and models handle natively.

Why modeling suits this rig

Pianoteq is a physical model rather than a sample player. Modartt cite a system footprint of about 40 MB that installs and loads in seconds. Sympathetic and duplex scale resonance, una corda, continuous half-pedal, repedaling, and the sound of the dampers returning to the strings are all computed continuously, not crossfaded between recordings. It also ships a velocity calibration tool that maps your specific controller, which matters more on a fifteen-year-old TP/40 than on a new keybed.

Pianoteq 8 and 9 packs cover Steinway D and B, Blüthner, Bechstein, Grotrian, Petrof, a set of upright models, and free historical instruments.

Settings that compensate for this keybed

Velocity. Use the plugin's calibration or curve editor so you reach full fortissimo without slamming and still control pianissimo. Start from the Nord curve you actually play and adjust from there, not from the plugin default.

Half-pedal. Enable continuous or partial pedal support, then read section 07, because the Triple Pedal will not feed it properly over MIDI.

Release. Set moderate note-off and release behaviour so repeated notes do not choke. In sampled libraries, reduce hard voice-cut on note-off.

05 / Rhodes and Wurli

The part you actually care about

On a heavy hammer action, modeled electric pianos generally beat sampled ones, for one reason: bark, growl, tine noise and bell tone change continuously with velocity instead of switching between recorded layers. On a keybed that is fighting you slightly, continuous is what you want.

Playing a Rhodes on the wrong action

Rhodes and Wurlitzer keybeds were light and semi-weighted. Yours is a hammer action designed to move a grand piano's worth of imaginary mass. The instruments were never meant to be played this way, and no plugin fully fixes that. What does help:

  1. Set the velocity curve in the plugin, not on the Nord, so the top layer arrives before you are hitting the keys hard. If you have to slam to get bark, the curve is wrong.
  2. Choose modeled over sampled where you can. A model degrades gracefully when your velocity distribution is compressed into the middle. A layered sample library exposes the seams.
  3. Check the low end specifically. Heavy actions push low velocities harder than you expect, and Rhodes bass turns muddy fast.

The signal chain, which is half the sound

Get the tremolo type right, because this is the most common mistake. A Rhodes has a stereo pan tremolo: the sound moves left and right. A Wurlitzer has a true amplitude tremolo: the volume pulses, in mono. Using the wrong one is instantly wrong to anyone who knows the instruments.

From there: preamp and drive, then tremolo or vibrato, then phaser (Small Stone or Phase 90 voicing) or chorus, then tape echo, spring reverb, and amp simulation. Fender Twin Reverb for a stage Rhodes, the Suitcase amp for a suitcase. Lounge Lizard, Stage-73 V and Wurli V all include usable versions of this chain, so third-party effects are optional rather than required.

06 / The computer

Headless instant-on, or just use the Lenovo

Your instinct about a dedicated box that turns on and is ready is right, and there is a well-documented answer for it. It is also narrower than a laptop, so the honest recommendation is both, for different jobs.

Raspberry Pi plus Pianoteq, the instant-on box

Pianoteq ships a native Linux ARM build and streams no samples, which makes it uniquely suited to a Pi. Documented builds in the wild:

  • A Pi 400 with a PreSonus Studio 24c at 44.1 kHz and a 64-sample buffer, roughly 1.5 ms, 256-voice polyphony, cold boot to fully playable in about 23 seconds.
  • A Pi 5 with a Pisound board, the community "Pianoberry" image from Blokas, tuned to around 1.33 ms.

Community scripts such as youfou/pianoteq-pi and Patchbox OS auto-launch Pianoteq headless as a systemd service, pin the CPU governor to performance, and can start it from a udev rule when the USB keyboard powers on. Configure once over HDMI or VNC, then run it blind. For a Rhodes and piano box that lives under the Nord and comes on with it, nothing else is this clean.

The tradeoff is real: it is a Pianoteq machine. No Kontakt, no Keyscape, no Ivory.

Making a Windows box behave like an appliance

If you go mini-PC rather than Pi, aim for a recent quad-core, 16 GB of RAM, and a fast NVMe SSD of 500 GB or more. Make it 32 GB if Keyscape, Ivory and Kontakt are all going to be loaded at once. Then:

  • Enable auto-login and put the host in the Startup folder, or schedule it with Task Scheduler.
  • Fit an HDMI dummy plug so the GPU initialises with no monitor attached.
  • Run VNC for the occasional configuration session.
  • Set the power plan to high performance, never sleep, and disable USB selective suspend so the interface and the Nord stay connected.
  • Defer Windows Update and cut background telemetry. Nothing ruins a take like a reboot prompt.

iPad, and the no-computer route

An iPad with Camelot Pro or AUM will host AUv3 instruments including B-3X and the iOS Rhodes and Wurlitzer apps. Connect the Nord by USB through a camera adapter and a powered hub. Nord forum users consistently report that a small USB interface such as a Tascam gives better latency and audio than going through Bluetooth, and Bluetooth MIDI should be avoided for playing entirely.

If you would rather skip computers: a second Nord (Electro or Stage), Dexibell, Viscount Legend '70s, Crumar Seven or Mojo, GSi Gemini or Burn, Korg SV-2, or Yamaha CP73 and CP88. More reliable on stage, less deep than Pianoteq or Keyscape, and considerably more expensive per sound.

Latency targets

Use a class-compliant interface with good drivers: PreSonus Studio, Focusrite Scarlett, MOTU M series, or RME if latency is the priority. Aim for round-trip under about 10 ms to feel comfortable, ideally 5 ms or lower. A 64 to 128 sample buffer at 44.1 or 48 kHz is the sweet spot. The Pi builds above sit near 1.33 to 1.5 ms, which is better than most laptop rigs will manage.

07 / Pedals over MIDI

Your triple pedal is better than what it transmits

On the instrument itself, the Nord Triple Pedal is excellent. Sustain on the right, sostenuto in the middle sending CC66, soft and una corda on the left sending CC67. The sustain pedal carries multiple sensors, so the Nord varies the amount and type of pedal noise on both the down and the up stroke, and supports half-pedaling on compatible pianos.

Partial fix, cheap

Over MIDI it is a different story. The generation-one Triple Pedal is a customised Fatar VFP3 built on a two-switch resistor network, so the CC64 it produces leaves the instrument as a small number of discrete stepped values rather than a smooth 0 to 127 sweep. The Nord's own engine reads those steps and synthesises pedal position and dynamic pedal noise internally, which is why it sounds so good on the board and so coarse in a plugin. Sostenuto and soft transmit effectively as on and off.

The fix

Feed the plugin from a separate continuous sustain pedal, a Roland DP-10, Yamaha FC3A, or Korg DS-1H, through a USB MIDI pedal interface such as an Audiofront MIDI Expression adapter. That outputs a genuine continuous CC64 straight to the host, bypassing the Nord entirely.

Keep the Triple Pedal plugged into the Nord for onboard sounds, where its stepped data and dynamic pedal noise are designed to work together. You end up with two pedals under the instrument and both of them doing what they are good at.

The alternative, if you already own a controller or interface that outputs continuous CC64, is to run the pedal into that and merge it with the Nord's note data in the host. Same result, no new purchase.

The uncomfortable part

An 88-note hammer action is a poor organ manual. No palm smears, no fast glisses, wrong bounce, wrong key resistance. Drawbars fix the tone and leave the touch exactly where it was.

The honest best answer, and what most players who care about organ end up doing, is a 61-key waterfall controller stacked above the Nord. Your drawbar controller then serves both manuals. If organ is a texture rather than a discipline for you, skip the second manual and buy the nanoKONTROL2; if you actually want to play organ, the second keyboard is the purchase that matters and the drawbars are secondary.

Synths on a hammer action

Same shape of problem, different missing pieces. The Nord sends no pitch bend, no modulation, and no aftertouch, so any expressive synth part is going to feel flat no matter which plugin you load. A small controller with wheels, an Arturia KeyLab or MiniLab, or an XY surface such as an Expressive E Touché, gets that expression back without displacing the Nord as your main keybed.

Tier 04

No limit

  1. Apple Silicon Mac mini with MainStage as the hub, running Pianoteq 9 with all packs, Keyscape, Ivory III, VI Labs, Noire and Una Corda, and B-3X. iPad with Logic Remote for control.
  2. RME interface for the lowest stable latency, targeting 5 ms round trip or better.
  3. Audiofront MIDI Expression with a high-quality continuous pedal, a GSi DMC-322, and a 61-key waterfall second manual.
  4. Preventative keybed service. Contact cleaning now beats a dead key in a session later.

Note Prices move constantly. Pianoteq, Keyscape, IK, Arturia and NI all run frequent sales.

Setup order, once the parts arrive

  1. Set Local Off on the Nord and assign a MIDI channel, so the internal engine and the plugin are not both sounding. This is the single most common cause of doubled notes and phantom velocity problems.
  2. Calibrate velocity in the plugin, not the board. Test that you can reach full fortissimo without slamming and still play genuinely quietly. Redo this per instrument; a Rhodes curve is not a grand piano curve.
  3. Route the continuous pedal through the USB MIDI interface for plugin work and leave the Triple Pedal on the Nord for onboard sounds.
  4. Enable continuous or partial pedal in each plugin, and turn on release samples and release velocity response wherever offered. The Nord does transmit release velocity, so use it.
  5. Lock the host down. Auto-login, host in Startup, HDMI dummy plug, high performance power plan, USB selective suspend off, updates deferred. On the Pi, systemd service, performance governor, and a udev rule to launch on keyboard power-up.
  6. Save a template with the Rhodes chain already built: preamp, correct tremolo type, phaser, tape echo, spring reverb, amp. Rebuilding that chain every session is how good ideas die.
10 / Confidence

Where this report is less certain

  • The stepped CC64 claim. Nord has never published the exact number or values of the discrete steps the generation-one Triple Pedal produces. The description here rests on well-supported community analysis of the NTP1 hardware and first-hand MIDI monitor reports, not an official specification. Put a MIDI monitor on your own unit and watch the CC64 stream while you press slowly. It takes two minutes and settles the question for your specific board.
  • Library v6 compatibility. Confirmed by Nord's own compatibility list, which starts at Nord Piano 3. If Nord ever backports, this changes. It has not so far.
  • Keybed faults. Contact wear, occasional double triggers and a single loud key are the most commonly reported long-term issues on Fatar actions of this era. Parts and service exist in 2026 but availability is thinning.
  • Prices. Approximate and volatile. Verify before buying, and never buy Arturia or IK at list.
  • Modeling versus sampling. Partly taste. Modeling wins on playability, footprint and pedal behaviour. Top sample libraries can still win on recorded room character for an exposed solo part. Demo both before committing.
  • The retrigger limit. Everything in section 02 mitigates and masks. Nothing eliminates it. If fast repetition is central to how you play, the honest answer is a triple-sensor keybed, and this instrument is not going to become one.
